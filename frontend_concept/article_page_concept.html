While the theatre changes, Accra's artists find new stages.

Renovation has emptied familiar rehearsal rooms at the National Theatre. The companies that worked inside are scattering across community halls, classrooms, and borrowed studios.

Published 31 Aug 2026 · 10:20 GMT 5 min read

In this report

01A landmark goes quiet
02Rehearsals move
03What returns

On a humid Monday evening, the actors are working through a scene in a borrowed room. Plastic chairs have been pushed against the walls. A portable speaker sits near the door. Every few minutes, the rehearsal pauses while someone crosses the corridor outside.

This is not the stage the company knows. For years, its rehearsals were shaped by the National Theatre's rooms, corridors, and familiar routines. With renovation underway, that work has moved into temporary spaces around Accra.

A landmark goes quiet

The National Theatre is both a building and an ecosystem. Performers arrive for rehearsals, technicians maintain equipment, costumiers move between rooms, and audiences gather long before the lights go down. Closing the building interrupts all of those rhythms at once.

Most artists support the need for renovation. Their uncertainty is about the period in between: where productions can develop, whether substitute venues can support the same work, and which smaller companies can absorb the additional cost of moving.

The stage is not only a room. It is the routine that allows people to make work together. A rehearsal director in Accra

Rehearsals move across the city

Some groups have found community halls. Others are using school rooms after classes, church spaces between services, and private studios offered by friends. Each room changes the work. A narrow floor alters movement; poor acoustics flatten dialogue; an early closing time shortens rehearsal.

The displacement is also creating unexpected connections. Companies that rarely shared space are exchanging contacts and equipment. Performers are meeting audiences in neighborhoods that may not usually travel to the theatre district.

What returns with the building

Renovation will eventually be judged by more than a restored exterior. Artists want rehearsal spaces that work, reliable technical systems, accessible public areas, and a building that can support both established companies and new performers.

For now, the work continues wherever a room can be found. The theatre may be quiet, but the people who gave it its sound have not stopped.

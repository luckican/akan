Analysis / Economy

Ghana is growing. The recovery is still uneven.

Technology and mining pushed the economy to its strongest first-quarter expansion in years. The headline number does not describe how that recovery feels—or who is receiving its benefits.

By Kofi Mensah Published 31 Aug 2026 · 07:40 GMT Updated 09:15 GMT 7 min read

In this report

01The headline figure
02Where growth came from
03What households feel

Ghana's economy expanded by 6.4% in the first quarter, a national figure strong enough to suggest that the recovery has gained real momentum. But the composition of that growth—and the distance between expanding sectors and household experience—deserves closer attention.

The largest push came from information and communication technology, which grew by 25.2%. Mining recovered by 10.7%, adding further weight to the headline figure. Together, a small number of high-performing sectors carried much of the acceleration.

The number is national. The experience is personal.

Gross domestic product measures the value created across the economy. It does not promise that the gains will arrive evenly, immediately, or in forms that households recognize. A fast-growing sector may employ relatively few people, concentrate income in particular regions, or rely heavily on imported equipment.

For many households, the recovery is judged through a different set of indicators: food prices, rent, transport costs, reliable work, and the value of wages after inflation. Those measures can move much more slowly than national output.

Growth answers how much the economy produced. It does not, by itself, answer who gained. Akan Reports analysis

Where the gains are concentrated

The technology expansion is important because it suggests continuing investment in connectivity, digital services, and business infrastructure. The next question is whether that expansion produces broad employment, stronger local companies, and productivity gains outside the largest cities.

Mining presents a similar distribution question. Higher production can support exports, taxes, and foreign-exchange earnings, while communities near extraction sites may still evaluate the sector through jobs, environmental costs, infrastructure, and local revenue.

What to watch next

The next releases should show whether growth is spreading into employment-intensive sectors and whether household consumption strengthens. Wage growth, food inflation, private-sector credit, and regional activity will help explain whether the recovery is becoming more widely felt.

The 6.4% figure is meaningful. The more consequential story is what happens after it: whether expanding sectors begin to change the material conditions through which people recognize economic progress.

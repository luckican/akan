# Akan Reports Content Strategy

**Version:** 1.5
**Status:** Approved MVP strategy
**Model:** Signal → Stakes → Follow-through

## Editorial promise

Akan Reports finds consequential developments in Ghana and the world around it, establishes the facts, traces where their weight lands and returns when the outcome becomes visible.

This strategy is a working editorial hypothesis for an experimental publication. It gives agents enough direction to select and shape stories without pretending the publication's identity can be perfected before real articles meet real readers.

The current positioning line, “Signals from Ghana's news cycle. Plus the world that shapes it,” is compatible with this strategy but is not a permanent tagline or a story-selection rule.

## Who we write for

Akan Reports writes for intelligent, curious non-specialists in Ghana and for readers elsewhere who want to understand Ghana and the world shaping it. They are interested in public life, money, culture, power and change, but they are not assumed to know an institution's internal language, an industry's professional roles or the history behind every continuing story.

The default register for straight news is restrained factual narration. Put the verified development first, attribute claims clearly, explain unfamiliar institutions or mechanisms only as needed, and let the order of the facts show the significance. Akan Reports does not need a visible narrator in every story. Use **we** when a genuine reporting check, synthesis, finding or evidence limit materially helps the reader. Use **you** when it genuinely clarifies the reader's stake. Neither is required.

The reporting may enter specialised subjects, but the assumed reader is not a regulator, executive, lawyer, analyst or communications professional. Institutions and industries are usually actors and sources, not the audience. Akan Reports should translate their language, establish the wider interest and give procedural detail only the space the story needs.

This does not mean simplifying away complexity or forcing every development into an immediate personal effect. A curious reader may value explanation, discovery, scale, tension, surprise, cultural meaning or a clearer view of how Ghana works. The form follows the reporting: some stories need a short Signal, some need sustained analysis or narrative, and some do not merit publication.

The audience definition is a starting point rather than a fixed demographic profile. It should be reviewed with evidence from real published work and reader behaviour.

## The editorial model

### 1. Signal

A Signal captures a verified new or newly relevant development using the amount of context it requires.

It should:

- identify a verified new or newly relevant development;
- state who acted, what they did, when, and where;
- add the immediate consequence, necessary context or next confirmed step only when it materially helps understanding;
- distinguish confirmed facts from claims and unknowns.

A Signal is not limited to social-media length. It may be short or substantial. The reporting decides the length.

Illustrative headlines:

- “Fuel prices fall again for September pricing window”
- “School placement results delayed to 4 September”
- “Parliament passes revised bill after committee changes”

Do not publish a Signal that merely paraphrases another publisher, reproduces a press release, or adds urgency without useful context.

### 2. Stakes

Stakes reporting is the publication's distinctive core. It examines consequences, tensions, distribution, power, winners, losers, and the part of a headline that a national total or official announcement does not answer.

It should:

- begin with a verified development or body of evidence;
- define the affected people, institutions, places, or decisions;
- explain the mechanism connecting the development to the consequence;
- show what the evidence can and cannot establish;
- ask a sharp question only when the reporting seriously examines it.

Illustrative headlines:

- “Ghana grows. Who gains?”
- “The cedi is stronger. Which prices should fall?”
- “Gold is rising. What reaches Ghana's public finances?”
- “A university loses accreditation. What happens to enrolled students?”

Stakes reporting must not manufacture conflict, infer motives without evidence, or force every story into a winners-versus-losers frame.

### 3. Context

Context explains a system, process, timeline, term, or recurring question that readers need in order to understand the news.

It should:

- answer a defined reader question;
- explain the process in a useful order;
- identify the responsible institutions and decision points;
- separate the general rule from exceptions and current uncertainties;
- be updated when the underlying facts or rules change.

Illustrative headlines:

- “What accreditation means for students already enrolled.”
- “How school placement works, and where families get stuck.”
- “Why cocoa prices abroad do not become farmer income directly.”
- “What happens after Parliament passes a bill?”

An explainer is not a glossary entry padded into an article. It needs a real reader need, current relevance, and verified detail.

### 4. World

World reporting covers an event outside Ghana only when Akan Reports can establish a meaningful Ghanaian connection or when the development materially shapes Ghana's choices, economy, security, environment, culture, or place in the world.

It should:

- state the outside development accurately;
- identify the mechanism through which it reaches Ghana;
- avoid forcing a Ghana connection that the evidence does not support;
- prioritize the Ghanaian consequence over a generic summary of world news.

Illustrative headlines:

- “Europe's cocoa rules are changing what Ghanaian farms must prove.”
- “A shipping disruption thousands of kilometres away is raising clearing costs in Tema.”
- “Why a change in US interest rates can affect Ghana's borrowing costs.”

General international coverage with no specific Ghanaian consequence does not qualify merely because it is important elsewhere.

### 5. Follow-through

Follow-through returns to a promise, policy, project, claim, investigation, or developing issue after an outcome can be checked.

It may include:

- government-policy development;
- project and budget follow-up;
- promise-versus-outcome reporting;
- timelines of a continuing issue;
- “what happened next?” updates.

Illustrative headlines:

- “Three budgets later, what happened to the railway project?”
- “The policy promised 10,000 jobs. How many exist?”
- “Six months after the rate cut, what happened to borrowing costs?”

Follow-through is part of the long-term identity but is not a standing MVP production obligation. The tracker remains deferred. Publish an ordinary follow-through article when the evidence is available; do not build a tracker or create analysis work merely to satisfy a quota.

## Angle framework

The strategy stream identifies the story's editorial job. The angle identifies the specific view through which the story performs that job.

Every agent-produced draft must choose one primary `editorial.angle_lens` and write one precise `editorial.angle` sentence. Supporting lenses may appear in the reporting. The primary lens should keep the reporting coherent, but it must not force a standard headline shape, section order, rhetorical question, or visible analytical scaffold.

### Approved angle lenses

#### `development`

**Question:** What is new or newly visible?

Use for a consequential new fact, decision, event, date, result, or update. State the change precisely, including who acted, when it applies, and the next confirmed step when that step is material.

This is the standard lens for an important story that everyone else is also reporting when Akan Reports can still provide a clear, verified, useful account.

#### `consequence`

**Question:** Who feels the effect, and how?

Trace the practical effect on households, workers, businesses, institutions, communities, or public services. Name the mechanism rather than asserting that an announcement will “impact Ghanaians.”

#### `distribution`

**Question:** Who gains, who bears the cost, and how is value or power divided?

Use when evidence can show how money, opportunity, risk, access, or decision-making power is distributed. Do not force a winners-versus-losers frame when the evidence does not support one.

#### `mechanism`

**Question:** How does this work?

Explain the chain between a decision and its outcome, the responsible institutions, the rules, the decision points, and where implementation can change the result.

#### `evidence`

**Question:** What do the records show compared with the public claim?

Compare an official, corporate, political, or popular claim with documents, data, direct evidence, and credible counterevidence. Do not begin with an assumption that the claim is false.

#### `delivery`

**Question:** What happened after the announcement or promise?

Check implementation, spending, completion, reach, or measurable results. Use for policies, budgets, programmes, projects, and institutional commitments.

#### `uncertainty`

**Question:** What material fact remains unknown, disputed, preliminary, or dependent on a future decision?

Use when identifying the evidence gap is itself useful to readers. Do not turn ordinary missing information into manufactured mystery.

#### `ghana-connection`

**Question:** Through what defensible mechanism does this outside development reach Ghana?

Use for global events involving trade, finance, regulation, security, climate, technology, migration, diplomacy, or culture. A passing mention of Ghana is not enough.

#### `next-step`

**Question:** What happens next, and what should be checked?

Identify the next decision, deadline, vote, release, implementation stage, measurement, or accountability point. Avoid predicting an outcome the evidence cannot support. Do not turn this lens into a stock closing paragraph.

### How to choose the angle

1. Begin with the strongest verified facts, not a preferred headline.
2. Identify which lens produces the clearest reader value from those facts.
3. Write the angle as one specific sentence naming the development, affected group, mechanism, evidence gap, or next decision.
4. Test whether the proposed headline, standfirst, and body can actually deliver that sentence.
5. Apply the scope test: every substantial section must help answer the headline or perform the primary angle. Remove or clearly subordinate material that does not; propose a separate story when the secondary development deserves substantial treatment of its own.
6. Change or narrow the angle when the evidence cannot support it.

An angle is not an opinion, a slogan, a mood, or a demand to disagree with other publishers. Distinctiveness can come from clarity, verification, source proximity, sequencing, consequence, context, synthesis, correction of confusion, or honest uncertainty. Added value does not require visible analysis in the prose.

When a widely reported development is important but no deeper lens is yet supportable, use `development` and publish a strong Signal. Include only the context, consequence, uncertainty or next confirmed step that the reader needs. Do not manufacture a Stakes angle or a standard “what happens next” ending.

If a candidate offers neither a useful `development` account nor another supported angle, it fails the added-value requirement and should return `NO DRAFT`.

### Worked example

For an illustrative central-bank policy-rate change:

| Strategy stream | Angle lens | Possible editorial angle |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | `development` | What rate changed, by how much, why the bank says it acted, and when the decision applies. |
| Stakes | `consequence` | Whether and when the change can reach the borrowing costs faced by households and businesses. |
| Context | `mechanism` | How the policy rate influences commercial lending and why the two rates do not move together immediately. |
| Follow-through | `delivery` | What happened to lending rates and credit conditions in the months after the decision. |

The agent chooses the version supported by the evidence available during that run, not the version that sounds most ambitious.

## MVP publishing mix

Use this mix to guide discovery across a body of work, not as a quota for a day or a command to publish weak material:

- **40% Signal:** keeps the publication current;
- **30% Stakes:** creates a recognizable editorial identity;
- **20% Context:** builds useful, searchable explanations;
- **10% World:** broadens the publication without losing focus.

Follow-through is published when evidence supports it and is not counted as an MVP quota. A Follow-through story may replace the closest matching stream in the mix.

Do not fabricate or lower the publication threshold to make the percentages balance. The human publisher may change the mix as real audience and production evidence develops.

## Strategy stream and story format

Every agent-produced article records one internal `editorial.strategy_stream`:

- `signal`
- `stakes`
- `context`
- `world`
- `follow-through`

The strategy stream describes the editorial job. The public `format` describes how the story is constructed:

| Strategy stream | Typical formats |
|---|---|
| Signal | `signal`, `report` |
| Stakes | `report`, `analysis`, `feature` |
| Context | `explainer`, `analysis` |
| World | `signal`, `report`, `analysis`, `explainer` |
| Follow-through | `report`, `analysis`, `explainer`, `feature` |

These are typical mappings, not exclusive ones. An agent must choose the combination that accurately describes the work.

The public sections `ghana`, `business`, and `world` are navigation destinations, not the strategy. A Business article can be a Signal, Stakes report, Context explainer, or Follow-through story. A strategy-stream value does not automatically determine homepage placement.

## Story-selection gate

Before researching a full draft, an agent must test a candidate against six questions:

1. **Newness:** Is something new, newly documented, newly relevant, or newly understandable?
2. **Consequence:** Does it affect money, power, opportunity, public services, daily life, culture, or Ghana's place in the world?
3. **Evidence:** Can the central facts be verified to the standard in `REPORTING_AND_QUALITY_RULES.md`?
4. **Added value:** Can Akan Reports improve the reader's understanding through stronger sourcing, clearer sequencing, correction of confusion, context, consequence, synthesis, explanation, or follow-through beyond the originating material?
5. **Angle:** Is there a precise reporting question or consequence rather than a broad topic?
6. **Reader fit:** Can an intelligent non-specialist follow the story and gain something beyond an update intended mainly for the institution or profession involved?

Evidence, added value and reader fit are mandatory. A candidate must also pass at least two of the other three questions. If it does not, the correct result is `NO DRAFT`.

A directed link is still only a lead. A popular or widely repeated item does not bypass the gate.

## Choosing among qualifying candidates

When autonomous discovery produces several candidates, prefer the one with the strongest combination of:

1. consequence for readers;
2. strength and proximity of evidence;
3. timeliness or newly relevant context;
4. distinctiveness of the Akan Reports angle;
5. usefulness after the first news cycle;
6. potential for meaningful follow-through.

An agent selects no more than one candidate per run unless the assignment explicitly requests otherwise. It may return `NO DRAFT` when none deserves publication.

## What Akan Reports does not publish by default

- rewrites that add no reporting value;
- press releases presented as independent reporting;
- unverified breaking claims;
- generic global news without a defensible Ghana connection;
- virality, outrage, celebrity attention, or social-media argument without meaningful consequence;
- vague commentary unsupported by reporting;
- predictions presented with more confidence than the evidence permits;
- articles produced only to fill a section, hit a word count, meet the publishing mix, or maintain an artificial schedule.

These are defaults, not a prohibition on subjects such as entertainment, sport, technology, or culture. Any subject may qualify when it passes the selection gate and delivers clear reader value.

## Sources and monitoring

The strategy does not lock Akan Reports to a fixed list of sources. Discovery agents may monitor a broad and evolving mix of official records, datasets, institutions, news organisations, specialist publications, local reporting, public statements, social platforms, and other open-web material.

Breadth in discovery does not reduce the verification standard. The material that reveals a lead may not be reliable enough to support the finished article.

## Learning period

This is an experimental MVP strategy. Evaluate it after the first 30 real published articles, using:

- which streams consistently produce publishable drafts;
- human rejection and revision reasons;
- search discovery and referral traffic;
- reader attention and return visits;
- which articles remain useful after the immediate cycle;
- production time, verification burden, and avoidable errors;
- whether the work is developing a recognizable identity.

Traffic and potential revenue matter to the project, but they are results to learn from rather than permission to publish weak or misleading work. Changes to the strategy require human approval and a version update to this file.

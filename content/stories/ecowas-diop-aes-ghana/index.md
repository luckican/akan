On 31 August 2026 in Abuja, retired Senegalese general Birame Diop took over as president of the ECOWAS Commission. The handover closed a four-year term under Omar Alieu Touray of The Gambia and installed a new statutory team for the 2026-2030 cycle.

Diop’s first public priority was not a new trade protocol. It was the break with Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger, which left ECOWAS and formed the Alliance of Sahel States (AES). In his inaugural remarks, carried by the Commission, he said West African integration still required open channels with those neighbours, including the AES, because shared security, connectivity, movement of people and cross-border projects do not stop at the new political line.

## What Diop said about the Sahel split

Speaking after the ceremony, Diop argued that withdrawal had not changed geography. Nigerian reporting of the same event quotes him saying ECOWAS and the AES countries still share about 5,250 kilometres of borders, and that terrorism, organised crime, irregular migration and environmental pressures cut across both spaces. “We have no other choice, because our brothers, our neighbours, our comrades have left us, but geography remains the same,” he said, according to THISDAY and Daily Trust.

Nigeria’s Minister of State for Foreign Affairs, Sola Enikanolaiye, speaking for President Bola Tinubu, backed deeper engagement with the AES states and pressed the new leadership for programmes that citizens can measure. That is a political signal from ECOWAS’s largest member, not yet a negotiated return of Burkina Faso, Mali or Niger to the bloc.

Nothing in the handover documents we reviewed sets a timetable, a negotiation format, or a list of projects that will restart across the ECOWAS-AES divide. The confirmed change is the Commission’s opening stance: keep talking and pursue pragmatic cooperation where interests overlap.

## Where Ghana sits in the new team

Among the incoming statutory appointees, the Commission named Ghana’s Amin Amidu Sulemani as commissioner for infrastructure, energy and digitalisation. The portfolio covers regional work on transport links, energy systems and digital transformation, the areas Touray’s outgoing team had already flagged in projects such as electricity interconnection and major corridor highways.

For Ghana, that seat is more than a diplomatic trophy. Regional power pools, road corridors and digital rules shape how goods and services move through a market that still includes Ghana after the Sahel departures. Whether Sulemani’s term delivers usable projects is a later test; the fact of Ghana holding the brief is the immediate institutional fact.

## Why the AES question lands in Accra

Ghana remains inside ECOWAS. It also faces the security and migration pressures that travel south from the Sahel toward the Gulf of Guinea. That is why Accra has been running its own track with the AES countries alongside the regional institutional fight in Abuja.

On 1 September 2026, The Herald Ghana reported that German Ambassador Frederik Landshöft met Colonel (Rtd.) Larry Gbevlo-Lartey, President John Dramani Mahama’s special envoy to the AES. Landshöft, the paper said, backed Ghana’s effort to rebuild dialogue on Sahel-Gulf of Guinea security cooperation and treated Ghana’s proposed Sahel-Gulf of Guinea Accord as a possible framework for joint work against terrorism, violent extremism and organised crime even when political differences remain. We did not find a separate German embassy statement in the sources checked for this article; the account rests on that meeting report.

For Ghana, the practical point is narrower than whether Burkina Faso, Mali or Niger rejoin ECOWAS. Security pressure still moves south toward the Gulf of Guinea, and regional power and road projects still cross the same geography the AES states left behind. Diop’s opening line keeps that neighbourhood on the Commission’s agenda. Ghana’s new commissioner seat puts Accra inside the brief that turns those corridors and power links into regional work. Neither fact settles the Sahel split. Together they show why a personnel change in Abuja is not only a West African institutional story for you in Ghana: the borders and the infrastructure did not leave with the politics.
